# Voice first, proof thin: what holds viewers

For a 30-40 second animated explainer-teaser, the best-evidenced retention lever is a voice that starts speaking in the first second and states what the video is about by second three. Everything else is weaker than the confidence with which it is usually taught. The only large dataset that isolates opening features, Emplifi's 2026 study of 10,110 Facebook brand Reels, ties speech in the first 3 seconds to **+11.7% retention at 3 s and +24.7% at 10 s** over music-only openings ([Emplifi](https://emplifi.io/resources/facebook-reels-data/)). Instagram and TikTok both officially ask for a hook inside 3 seconds, but neither publishes an effect size. Roughly **two in three Reel plays are swiped away before second 3** and the average Reel is watched for about 8.5 seconds, so the opening and the first 10 seconds decide most of the outcome ([Socialinsider](https://socialinsider.io/blog/instagram-reels-statistics/); [Metricool](https://metricool.com/press-release-instagram-study-2026/)). Camera moves, transitions, sound effects, risers, beat sync, scoreboards and loops have no retention measurement at all on short-form video; they rest on lab findings about attention, or on craft convention. Several popular rules are contradicted outright: the "comment a keyword" call to action, the Zeigarnik effect, the "85% watch muted" figure, and "faster is always better". No study found tests animated or 3D characters, and none is specific to Indonesian audiences, so every technique below is a transfer from live-action, ads, film or lab work.

## Twenty-two techniques, ranked by evidence and likely payoff

The ranking weighs two things: how good the evidence is, and how much of the audience the technique touches (anything acting in the first 3-10 seconds reaches far more viewers than anything at the end). Grades mean: **strong** = primary platform statement or solid peer-reviewed result; **moderate** = large stated sample but correlational, or peer-reviewed work from an adjacent format; **weak** = indirect, small, or qualitative platform advice; **unverified** = craft convention or vendor claim with no traceable data. A strong grade on a platform statement means the platform said it, not that an effect size exists.

| 5 | Hold the shot when the voice delivers a number, name or claim. Cut fast only where narration is light. | Camera | Moderate | TV research: fast pace plus arousing content lowers recall of spoken content |

Whip pans, speed ramps, risers, beat-synced cuts, on-screen scoreboards and progress bars, mid-video "pattern interrupts", seamless loops at this length, and follow calls to action are unranked. Nothing found measures any of them against retention. They are discussed in the sections below and in the unsupported-claims table.

## Speech by frame one beats every other hook feature

The platforms agree on timing and stay silent on magnitude. Instagram tells creators to "start with a compelling hook in the first 3 seconds" ([Instagram Creators](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/helping-creators-of-all-sizes-break-through)). TikTok separates two deadlines: proposition in the first 3 seconds, hook inside the first 6 ([TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en)). A Marketing Science model of TikTok influencer ads puts the engagement peak in the first 6 seconds, which fits a 0-6 second hook window better than a hard 3-second cliff ([Yang et al.](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2021.0107)).

Among measurable opening features, **speech dominates**. In the Emplifi data a person on screen in the first 3 seconds adds only +3.6% at 3 s and +10.1% at 10 s, then turns to -2.4% at 30 s. Text overlay adds +5.1% at 3 s overall. The hook gets people past second 10; it does not carry the video. The same dataset shows speech and person hooks with slightly lower reach (-3.3% and -7.4%), a correlational wrinkle that most likely reflects content mix, and its body text and tables disagree in the second decimal, so the numbers are directional ([Emplifi](https://emplifi.io/resources/facebook-reels-data/)).

For the verbal hook, lab work supports a specific shape. Curiosity is highest at a moderate knowledge gap, where the viewer knows enough to see exactly what is missing ([Journal of Cognition](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.501)). That reconciles the open loop with "proposition in 3 seconds": name the topic and withhold the outcome. A hook that hides the subject too opens a gap too wide to care about, and headline field experiments show curiosity framing produces mixed, sometimes opposing results ([PMC11704130](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11704130/)). No field study compares question, bold claim, contrast or "I gave X to Y" framings against hold rate. Rankings of hook formulas are vendor material.

The presenter-in-frame choice is the least certain part of the hook. Face presence raises engagement on brand TikToks, more so when the face is larger and closer ([Wallach et al.](https://www.emerald.com/jcm/article-abstract/42/3/318/1247037/Analyzing-the-impact-of-faces-on-consumer)). That is human faces and likes, not a stylised 3D character and 3-second hold. If the character opens the video, a large, close, to-camera framing is the reading the evidence supports. "Open on motion" is plausible and unmeasured.

## The cut-rate ceiling is the voice-over, not the eye

No platform document or large-sample study states an optimal interval between visual changes. TikTok's own material says "fast scene changes draw users in early" and attaches no number ([TikTok Creative Codes](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/Creative_Codes_ENG.pdf)). The "pattern interrupt every 2-4 seconds" rule appears only in vendor posts that disagree with each other. It most plausibly restates feature-film practice, where a viewpoint changes every 2.7-5.4 seconds ([Smith & Henderson 2008](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/download/2264/3460/8488)). A change every 2-4 seconds is a reasonable default, not a finding.

Two results push against speed as a goal. Emplifi ties slower pacing to about **+7.8% retention at 30 s** ([Emplifi](https://emplifi.io/resources/facebook-reels-data/)). Television research finds that fast pace combined with arousing content lowers recall of specifics, with memory for spoken content falling as visual memory holds ([Lang et al.](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2148789875)). For an explainer whose message lives in the narration, the practical rule is to speed the picture where the words are light and hold it where a fact lands.

Transitions have perceptual evidence and no retention evidence. Viewers told to spot every edit still missed **32.4% of match-action cuts** against 9.4% of between-scene cuts, and misses came from following the story, not from blinks ([Smith & Henderson 2008](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/download/2264/3460/8488)). So a cut on continuing action keeps one event flowing, and a hard scene cut reads as "new thing". A whip pan fits the conditions under which cuts go unnoticed, but nobody has tested one. Speed ramps have no study of any kind. Decorative wipes and glitches have no evidence either way; their only cost is seconds.

Camera motion follows the same pattern. Motion and flicker are the best low-level predictors of where everyone looks ([Mital et al. 2011](https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/rhill2/pubs/Mital_(2011)_Clustering_of_gaze_during_dynamic_scene_viewing.pdf)). Looming motion captures attention, though later work credits motion onset more than direction ([review](https://www.scielo.br/j/pn/a/VDmtz96hS9yVTyrtdh6DFMJ/?lang=en)). The usable inference is to start a move on a beat, since a constantly drifting camera supplies no new onset. Claims that a dolly beats a zoom, that parallax helps, or that a lighting recipe raises attention have no source. Several film-cognition figures in the research were read only through abstracts or summaries and should be treated as directional.

## Sound has lab backing for sync, silence and separation

Platforms say build sound-on and publish little that bears on retention. Meta's figure is the useful base rate: **over 75% of Instagram Reels views are sound-on** ([Meta for Developers](https://developers.facebook.com/blog/post/2024/11/07/unlock-the-power-of-reel-ads/)). No platform publishes anything about whooshes, risers, impacts or effect density.

Three mechanisms have real support from outside short-form. A sound synchronised with a visual change makes that change pop out in lab search tasks ([Van der Burg et al. 2008](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/2321477/Burg%20Journal%20of%20Vision%208(5)%202008%20u.pdf)), but the memory benefit comes from the sound meaning the same thing as the picture, and sync violations barely affect recall ([congruency study](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1777983849)). A right-sounding effect a frame late beats a wrong-sounding one on the frame. Second, new audio elements trigger an orienting response that **fades after the third repetition** of the same effect, while voice changes resist habituation ([Potter et al. 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6634355/)). Third, cutting the music to silence just before a key line improved recall in radio ads, provided the gap was 3 seconds or less ([Olsen 2002](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/salient-stimuli-in-advertising-the-effect-of-contrast-interval-le)).

Voice level has the firmest numbers. Broadcast listening tests recommend at least 10 LU between commentary and music and 15 LU between commentary and ambience, and ordinary listeners preferred about 4 LU more separation than experts ([Fraunhofer](https://publica.fraunhofer.de/entities/publication/67a38de8-7a6d-422b-93b4-180552acfd89)). These come from loudspeaker TV listening, so phone playback argues for the upper end. The -14 LUFS target is a convention: YouTube's reference is community-measured, and TikTok and Instagram publish nothing ([Production Advice](https://productionadvice.co.uk/youtube-loudness/)).

Music choice has the least support. Fast tempo raised purchase intent for gain-framed ads only, and only above a tempo threshold; it says nothing about completion ([Journal of Advertising Research](https://thearf.org/access-knowledge-2/publications/journal-of-advertising-research/when-fast-music-makes-ads-more-persuasive-and-when-it-doesnt/)). Beat-synced editing and risers have no quantitative evidence at all.

## Instagram rewards sends and penalises keyword comments

Instagram's head named the top three ranking signals as watch time, likes and sends, with sends mattering slightly more for non-follower reach ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/)). Comments and follows are absent. TikTok says finishing a video is a strong signal and that follower count is not a direct factor ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). No platform publishes weights.

The comment call to action carries a documented risk. Instagram said content may not be recommended if it explicitly asks for engagement, and gave as its example "asking people to comment with a specific word, number, or emoji" ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-advice-on-single-word-ctas-and-longer-reels/718151/)). The same statement exempts earnest interaction and says automation tools such as ManyChat are not the target, and Meta's Facebook guideline exempts asking people whether they support an issue ([Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/approach-to-ranking/content-distribution-guidelines/engagement-bait/)). A two-option vote sits in a grey zone: "comment 1 or 2" matches the named example, while "which side would you pick, and why?" reads as a real question. Even a compliant prompt has no evidence of raising reach. Its defensible purpose is audience investment and material for part 2.

Cliffhangers and series are tolerated but unproven. Instagram shipped a "Link a Reel" button for multi-part content and published no reach data ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/instagram-reels-linking-feature-encourages-multi-part-series)). The only controlled experiment, on TV drama with 133 participants, found cliffhangers raise arousal but not enjoyment or intention to keep watching ([Wirz et al. 2023](https://folia.unifr.ch/unifr/documents/319990)). Whether a "follow for part 2" ending changes follow rate is unknown, and so is backlash to withheld payoffs.

On-screen devices split cleanly. Text and captions have some support, with the caveat already noted for speech-led video. Seamless loops lifted replays by 18.7%, but only on Reels of 7 seconds or less, and the effect fades with length ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/human-speech-and-presence-help-drive-reels-engagement/815381/)). At 30-40 seconds the last frame is better spent on the part-2 message. Progress bars, timers, counters and scoreboards have no credible measurement. A scoreboard can still make the open question concrete, which the curiosity research favours, but that is a design argument.

Length is the one structural worry the data removes. In 9.77 million brand Reels from the first half of 2026, the 30-60 second bucket had the highest reach rate at 5.60%, against 5.20% under 30 seconds ([Socialinsider](https://socialinsider.io/blog/instagram-reels-statistics/)). The gap is small and correlational, so it reads as "no penalty".

## Ten popular rules the evidence rejects or cannot find

| Claim | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Comment KEYWORD" is a safe growth device on Instagram | Contradicted | Instagram named the pattern as content it may not recommend ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-advice-on-single-word-ctas-and-longer-reels/718151/)) |
| The Zeigarnik effect proves unfinished stories stick | Contradicted | A 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks; only the tendency to resume holds ([Ghibellini & Meier](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)) |
| Cliffhangers make people want to keep watching | Contradicted (TV) | More arousal, no rise in intent to continue ([Wirz et al.](https://folia.unifr.ch/unifr/documents/319990)) |
| 85% of viewers watch muted | Not applicable | A 2016 publisher anecdote about muted-autoplay Facebook feed ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/)); Meta reports over 75% of Reels views sound-on |
| Captions raise completion by 80% (or 40%) | Misquoted | 80% is the share of survey respondents who said they would be more likely to finish ([Streaming Media](https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/News/Online-Video-News/80-of-Video-Caption-Users-Arent-Hearing-Impaired-Finds-Verizon-131860.aspx)) |
| Faster cuts and faster pacing always hold better | Contradicted | Slower pacing tied to +7.8% at 30 s ([Emplifi](https://emplifi.io/resources/facebook-reels-data/)); fast pace costs recall of speech |
| A pattern interrupt is needed every 2-4 s (or within 0.8 s) | Unverified | Vendor posts with conflicting intervals ([Opus Clip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-hook-formulas)); no primary source |
| Always add on-screen text | Contradicted for voiced video | Negative at 10 s and 30 s on speech-led Reels ([Emplifi](https://emplifi.io/resources/facebook-reels-data/)) |
| Seamless loops boost replays | Over-generalised | Measured only at 7 s or less |
| Trending audio boosts reach; -14 LUFS is a platform rule | Unverified | A secondary report has Instagram denying any boost for music ([Kontentino](https://www.kontentino.com/q-and-a/trending-audio-instagram-reach/)); no platform loudness spec exists |

Also untraceable: universal hold-rate benchmarks (YouTube tells creators to compare against their own similar videos, [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?hl=en&co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts)), "a face raises retention 35%", "first-frame text lifts hold 50%", "3.2x more follows with a part 2", "zoom is 38% less immersive than a dolly", and "animated captions add 34% watch time". The face and text figures are roughly ten times Emplifi's measured +3.6% and +5.1%.

## Conclusion

The evidence sorts the four areas into a clear order of confidence. The hook has field data. Structure has platform statements. Sound has lab and broadcast mechanisms. Camera and effects have perception science only. That order is close to the reverse of where production effort usually goes on a 3D piece. The measured gains sit in script decisions that cost nothing to render: when the voice starts, what the first sentence says, how much text competes with it, and how the closing question is worded. Camera polish and sound design still matter for comprehension and for directing the eye, but they should be judged against the video's own retention graph, not justified with statistics that do not exist.

Because every number here is correlational and borrowed from brand video, ads, film or radio, the account's own metrics are the only real test. Instagram's skip rate and retention chart, and YouTube's "viewed vs swiped away" and engaged views, measure the hook directly. Instagram's trial reels, shown only to non-followers, are the official way to compare two openings. The highest-value experiment is the one the evidence is most divided on for this format: a keyword-comment ending against a genuine-question ending, with sends per reach and non-follower reach as the outcome.
